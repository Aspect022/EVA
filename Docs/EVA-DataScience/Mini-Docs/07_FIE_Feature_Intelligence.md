# FIE — Feature Intelligence Engine
### EVA System Specification | Data Science Module
**Version 2.0**

---

## 1. Purpose

The Feature Intelligence Engine is responsible for identifying what additional variables would make the data more analytically meaningful — and explaining why.

Raw columns are rarely the best representation of what matters. A dataset containing a customer's first purchase date and today's date does not directly contain a "days since first purchase" variable — but that variable is likely far more useful than either raw date alone. A dataset containing height and weight does not contain BMI — but BMI may capture something about health risk that neither raw measurement does individually.

The FIE's job is to think about what information is latent in the raw columns, what the real-world domain suggests would be meaningful to derive, and what the user's analytical goal indicates would be useful — and then to propose those derived variables with clear reasoning about both their analytical value and their real-world meaning.

---

## 2. Design Principle

Feature engineering is a creative act, but it is not an arbitrary one. The variables that get derived must be motivated by something: domain knowledge, analytical theory, or a specific hypothesis generated by the IHE.

In typical automated systems, feature engineering is either skipped entirely or performed generically — creating interaction terms, polynomial expansions, and time lags without asking whether they are meaningful. Generic features produce noise. Domain-specific features produce signal.

The FIE operates from a simple rule: **every proposed feature must have a real-world meaning that a domain expert would recognize.** A feature that is statistically derived but has no interpretable meaning in the domain is not a good feature. It may appear to improve a metric, but it cannot be explained, and an unexplainable feature leads to unexplainable conclusions — which violates EVA's core commitment to transparency.

The corollary: **features that are meaningful in one domain may be meaningless or misleading in another.** The FIE always calibrates to the specific domain, not to a general playbook.

---

## 3. Position in the Pipeline

**Comes after:** IHE (Stage 4). The hypotheses generated by the IHE are a primary source of feature ideas — if a hypothesis proposes that "customer disengagement" explains churn, a feature capturing engagement level (frequency of logins, days since last activity) is a natural derivation.

**Comes before:** VPE (Stage 6). The derived features may produce new and more informative visualizations than the raw columns.

**Rule:** The FIE must read all prior GAL sections before proposing features. Column restrictions from DRIL must be respected. The analytical goal from QBII must shape which features are worth creating.

---

## 4. Inputs

**From the GAL:**
- Section 1 — Dataset Identity (column roles, domain, row meaning, time behavior — the raw material and context)
- Section 2 — User Intent (analytical goal and target — determines what types of features are relevant)
- Section 3 — Data Integrity Record (restricted columns — features may not be derived from these)
- Section 4 — Exploratory Findings (patterns identified during exploration — may suggest useful transformations)
- Section 5 — Hypotheses (proposed real-world mechanisms — the richest source of feature ideas)

---

## 5. Core Responsibilities

1. Identify what information is latent in existing columns but not directly available
2. Propose derived features motivated by domain knowledge, exploratory findings, or hypotheses
3. Justify each feature with both a statistical rationale and a real-world meaning
4. Ensure no features are derived from restricted columns
5. Estimate the expected analytical value of each feature
6. Record all proposals and reasoning in the GAL

---

## 6. Process

### Step 1 — Read Context and Map Available Material

The FIE begins by reviewing what columns exist, what roles they have been assigned, which are restricted, and what the domain and user intent establish as relevant.

From this, it forms a mental inventory: what raw material is available to work with, and what is off-limits.

### Step 2 — Read Hypotheses as Feature Prompts

The hypotheses in Section 5 of the GAL are the FIE's most valuable input. Each hypothesis proposes a real-world mechanism. That mechanism implies what variables would be needed to capture it.

If a hypothesis proposes that "customer disengagement drives churn," the relevant mechanism implies that engagement should be measurable. The FIE asks: can engagement be derived from existing columns? If a last-activity date exists, "days since last activity" is a direct operationalization of the concept. If purchase frequency is recorded, "average days between purchases" is another.

The FIE works through the hypothesis list systematically, asking for each: what variable would help evaluate or capture this mechanism?

### Step 3 — Apply Domain Knowledge

Beyond hypotheses, domain knowledge provides another layer of feature ideas. Different domains have established practices around what variables are analytically meaningful.

**Examples by domain:**

In customer behavior datasets, variables that capture recency, frequency, and monetary value (how recently a customer purchased, how often, and how much) are well-established signals.

In time series or sensor datasets, rolling averages, rolling variances, and lag variables (the value of a measurement at a prior time point) capture trend and change information that point-in-time values miss.

In healthcare datasets, grouping continuous measurements into clinically meaningful categories (BMI categories, age groups corresponding to life stages) often captures threshold effects that raw numbers miss.

In HR datasets, tenure (time since hire date) and time since last promotion or review are often more predictive than raw hire dates.

The FIE applies domain-specific knowledge to identify what variables a skilled analyst in that field would typically create.

### Step 4 — Identify Transformation Opportunities

Beyond creating entirely new variables, some existing columns can be made more useful through transformation.

**Binning or categorization** — converting a continuous variable into meaningful categories. This is appropriate when the relationship between the variable and the outcome is not linear. Age in years may be less useful than age group (child, young adult, middle-aged, senior) if outcomes vary by life stage rather than by precise age.

**Date decomposition** — extracting meaningful components from date columns. A purchase date contains year, month, day of week, week of year, and time since a reference date. Some of these are more useful than the raw date.

**Ratio features** — expressing two related measurements as a ratio. Revenue per customer, cost per unit, clicks per impression. Ratios often capture the relationship between two quantities more cleanly than either alone.

**Interaction features** — combining two columns to capture a concept that neither represents alone. These require careful motivation — they should be proposed only when there is a specific real-world reason to expect the interaction to matter, not as a mechanical expansion of the feature space.

### Step 5 — Evaluate and Filter

Not every potential feature that could be derived is worth proposing. The FIE filters by asking:

- Does this feature have a clear real-world meaning that a domain expert would recognize?
- Is this feature motivated by a specific hypothesis, exploratory finding, or established domain practice?
- Is this feature derivable from columns that are not restricted?
- Does this feature add information that is not already captured by existing columns?

Features that fail any of these tests are not proposed. The FIE aims for a focused, motivated set of features rather than a large generic one.

---

## 7. Output Written to the GAL

The FIE writes Section 6 — Feature Reasoning. For each proposed feature:

- **Feature Name** — a clear, descriptive name
- **Source Columns** — which raw columns are used to derive it
- **Derivation Logic** — how the feature is computed, described in plain language (not code)
- **Real-World Meaning** — what this feature represents about the domain, in terms a non-technical reader would understand
- **Statistical Purpose** — what information it captures that the source columns do not directly represent
- **Motivating Source** — whether this feature was motivated by a hypothesis (with reference), an exploratory finding (with reference), or established domain practice
- **Expected Analytical Value** — what the feature is likely to contribute to the analysis
- **Limitations** — any risks or conditions under which this feature might mislead

---

## 8. Failure Handling

**Domain is ambiguous and domain-specific features are hard to justify:** The FIE falls back to general analytical feature types — recency, frequency, and ratio features — that are broadly applicable. It records that domain-specific features were limited by domain ambiguity.

**All hypotheses are at low plausibility:** The FIE still proposes features based on exploratory findings and domain knowledge. Low-plausibility hypotheses can still motivate useful features — the plausibility assessment reflects evidence, not the feature's potential value.

**Restricted columns are the most informative ones:** The FIE records this as a significant limitation in the GAL. It cannot work around restrictions; it can only note that better features would be available if certain restrictions did not apply, and explain why the restrictions are in place.

---

## 9. Operational Rules

1. Features may not be derived from restricted columns identified in Section 3 of the GAL.
2. Every proposed feature must have a real-world meaning expressible in plain language.
3. Every proposed feature must be motivated — by a hypothesis, a finding, or established domain practice.
4. Interaction features require explicit motivation; they may not be proposed generically.
5. The FIE does not propose features for modeling performance without real-world justification. Performance without interpretability is not EVA's goal.
6. All feature proposals are recorded in the GAL regardless of whether they are ultimately used.
